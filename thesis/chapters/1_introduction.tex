\section{Inkwell Data Ltd}
This thesis project was carried out during a six-month internship at
\textit{Inkwell Data Ltd}, as part of the \textit{Master's Degree} program in
\textit{Computer Science} at the \textit{University of Padua}.  
\textit{Inkwell Data Ltd} a company operating in the \textit{Industrial
Internet of Things} (\textit{IIoT}) sector, specializing in connectivity
solutions and digital-twin technologies for industrial assets.\\
The concept of digital twin was first envisioned in 1991, although the term
\textit{'digital twin'} was officially used in 2010 by the
\textit{NASA}~\cite{glaessgen2012digital} and has, since then, become widely
adopted across many industries. In the context of this thesis, a \textit{digital
twin} is understood as a digital model of a physical object and its associated
processes and systems, which needs to dynamically reflect the state of the
real-world object it represents by being continuously connected to it for data
exchange throughout its lifecycle.  

Founded in 2019, with teams interconnecting London and Padua, the company
started with \textit{Altior}, a middleware \textit{Platform as a Service}
(\textit{PaaS}) designed to simplify the creation and management of digital
twins at an enterprise scale.

At the core of \textit{Altior}, device twin instances are treated as data
structures reflecting the current state of the corresponding real-world object,
allowing the platform to remain highly responsive even under heavy load. Rather
than focusing on the mere collection of data, \textit{Altior} places particular
emphasis on how this data flows and is processed once collected, keeping the
digital instance of the device up to date with the state inferred from the
received data.  

This focus on data flow is achieved by design: \textit{Altior} is architected
as a strongly distributed system. The platform supports vertical scaling, by
allocating additional resources to the running system, as well as horizontal
scaling, as its runtime environment can be transparently replicated across
one or more servers within a cluster, providing load balancing and fault
tolerance, all while maintaining a strong emphasis on security. 

On top of the \textit{Altior} platform, \textit{Inkwell Data Ltd} develops
\textit{Software as a Service} (\textit{SaaS}) applications tailored to specific
industrial domains. One such application is \textit{Swarm}, a product primarily
focused on the water metering sector, although the company's technology is
designed to generalize to a broader range of connected industrial devices.
Following a design that reflects current trends in distributed systems,
adopted to achieve independent scalability, fault isolation and logical
decoupling between components, \textit{Swarm} is organized as an Elixir umbrella
application, structured following microservices principles into a set of
independent applications.

\section{Context and Motivation}
Historically, software systems were designed as monolithic applications
deployed on a single server, where all components, business logic, and data
access layers resided within a single process. This architecture simplified
development and deployment but imposed intrinsic limitations: scalability was
constrained to the vertical addition of resources to a single machine, and a
failure in any component could compromise the availability of the entire
system. The growing scale and availability requirements of modern software
systems, together with a user base that is increasingly global rather than
local, have made distributed architectures the standard way of building large
applications. By partitioning computation and data across multiple
interconnected nodes, these architectures make it possible to scale with the
workload, to tolerate hardware and software failures and to serve geographically
dispersed requests with acceptable response times.

Among distributed architectures, \textit{microservices} have become one of the
most widely adopted style: the application is decomposed into small, loosely
coupled services, each responsible for a specific business capability and
independently deployable and scalable. To preserve this independence, services
commonly adopt the \textit{database per service} pattern, according to which
each service owns its data and exposes it only through its own interface. Since
every service is free to choose the storage technology best suited to its needs,
the data of a single logical application ends up fragmented across multiple,
independently managed, and often heterogeneous data sources, which differ in
data model, query language, and storage technology. This fragmentation is not
peculiar to microservices, but is a recurring characteristic of distributed
systems in which data is spread across heterogeneous data sources, representing
the context in which this thesis, with the design and implementation
\textit{DEX}, operates.

\section{Problem Statement and Objectives}
The shift toward distributed architectures, most notably through the adoption of
the \textit{database-per-service} pattern in microservices, inevitably leads to
the fragmentation of application data across multiple, independently managed,
and highly heterogeneous data sources. 

This structural characteristic of distributed architectures particularly
affects those who need to query the data of the system. The queries that an
application can serve are, in general, those anticipated and implemented by its
developers at design time. Any new information need that requires combining the
data of several services or components in an unforeseen way cannot be satisfied
without modifying and redeploying part of the system, which couples the agility
of data access to the development lifecycle of the software. Even when direct
access to the data sources is available, answering such a need remains costly:
it requires knowledge of the different technologies and query languages adopted
by each source, beyond the effort of aggregating the partial results retrieved
from each of them into a coherent answer. 

This limitation is amplified by the profile of the end users, who are not
necessarily technical personnel able to operate with these technologies: they
are often domain experts, who may be capable of delivering value from data while
being unable to write queries in the languages of the underlying data sources.
Whenever an ad-hoc query is needed, users must rely on the team responsible for
the product to implement it on their behalf. When the data is spread across
multiple components owned by different teams, the request may additionally
require coordination among all of them. The result is a long delay between the
formulation of an information need and its fulfillment, together with a
recurring maintenance burden for the development teams, who are repeatedly asked
to implement one-off queries outside the scope of the predefined interfaces of
the system. 

The objective of this thesis is to design and develop \textit{DEX}, a system
that allows users to explore and query multiple heterogeneous and distributed
data sources, without requiring modifications to the sources nor knowledge of
the query language or technology adopted by each of them.
In particular, this work pursues the following objectives. First, to define a
uniform query interface that abstracts over the heterogeneity of the data
sources, so that information needs can be expressed without knowing where or how
the relevant data is stored. Second, to support \textit{data exploration},
allowing users to approach the data iteratively, starting from a broad question
and progressively refining it through successive queries, without having to know
in advance exactly which data they need. Third, to provide a layer of
\textit{data abstraction and governance}: users interact with a logical model
that decouples them from the physical schema of each source, and, since every
query is mediated by the system, access policies can be defined once, at the
level of this model, enforcing uniformly across all the underlying sources,
regardless of how each of them manages its own permissions. Fourth, to design a
distributed architecture implementing \textit{federated query processing}, able
to decompose a single logical query into subqueries executable against the
individual data sources, and to reconcile their partial results into a coherent
response. Fifth, to ensure that adding a new data source, or changing an
existing one, limits the need for new development to what is strictly necessary,
relying as much as possible on configuration. Finally, to evaluate the system in
terms of performance and scalability, in order to assess its viability for
querying data distributed across heterogeneous data sources, such as those found
in microservices architectures.

\section{Thesis Structure}
This thesis is organized into seven chapters. The background, the
requirements and design goals guiding the development of the framework are
covered first, followed by the architecture and implementation of \textit{DEX}.
The system is then evaluated, with the thesis concluding with the main 
contributions and some possible directions for future work. The chapters are 
organized as detailed below: 

\begin{description}
      \item[{\hyperref[chap:background]{\textbf{Chapter 2}}}] provides the
      background required to understand the project. It introduces data
      exploration, distributed system concepts, heterogeneous data sources, data
      abstractions, data governance and distributed query processing. The chapter
      concludes with an overview of existing solutions and technologies related
      to the presented problem; 
      \item[\textbf{Chapter 3}] describes the requirements and design goals of
      \textit{DEX}. It defines the functional requirements, quality
      requirements, and constraints that guided the development of the system;
      \item[\textbf{Chapter 4}] presents the architecture of \textit{DEX}. It
      first provides an overview of the system and its main architectural
      principles, and then describes its adoption of the \textit{Hexagonal
      Architecture}. The chapter also discusses distributed execution, fault
      tolerance, and the application of microservice principles within the
      \textit{BEAM} environment; 
      \item[\textbf{Chapter 5}] describes the implementation of \textit{DEX}. It 
      introduces the main technologies adopted by the system and details the
      implementation of query processing, data source integration, persistence,
      configuration, and client interactions. The chapter also discusses the
      concrete mechanisms used to provide fault tolerance;
      \item[\textbf{Chapter 6}] evaluates the developed system. It describes the
      experimental setup and presents the results of the performance and
      scalability evaluations. The obtained results are subsequently discussed
      to assess the behavior of \textit{DEX} under the considered experimental
      conditions;
      \item[\textbf{Chapter 7}] concludes the thesis by summarizing the main
      contributions of the project and discussing its current limitations. The
      chapter concludes by presenting possible directions for future
      development.
\end{description}
