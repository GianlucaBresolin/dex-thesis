\chapter{Background}\label{chap:background}

\section{Microservices}
The adoption of distributed architectures has been driven by a combination of
needs: the ability to scale to sustain growning workloads, fault tolerance in
the presence of hardware and/or software failures, and, with software products
increasingly reaching a global rathen than local user base, the ability to serve
geographically dispersed requests with acceptable response times. \\
A single server running a monolithic application, with all components,
business logic and data access layers residing within a single process, is a
solution which is unable to satisfy these needs beyond a certain scale. Any
concurrency it is able to offer remains bounded by the capacity of that single
machine, which constitutes both a bottleneck and a single point of failure,
potentially compromising the availability of the entire system. Moreover, being
tied to a fixed physical location, it is unable to satisfy strict latency
requirements for all users.\\
In this context, sustaining higher workloads requires to scale vertically, by
adding more powerful or additional components, but this infrastructural approach
tends to incur into costs which grow exponentially in relation to the amount of
load that the system is able to support, making it an unsustainable choice for
lage-scale data and user applications.\\ 

The natural step was to scale horizzontally, distributing the workloads across
multiple, independent and typically less expensive machines rather than
concentrating it on a single, powerful and costly one. However, just replicating
a monolithic application across several nodes, doesnn't solve all the previous
problems: a software or hardare failure will potentially cause sessions or
application state issues. Moreover, different monoliths will still point to the
same database, becoming a single point of failure and a performance bottleneck.
Finally, application logic components are replicated equally, even if they
might require different scalability dynamics, leading to an unefficient use of
the available resources. 

A further limitation of the monolith concerns the isolation among its parts:
monoliths are commonly associated with the informal expression \textit{big ball
of mud} due to the absence of clear boundaries among their components. Indeed,
their dependencies are complex and tangled, leading to a codebase which is hard
to understand and to modify. A possible way to introduce isolation is the
\textit{Service Oriented Architecture} (SOA)~\cite{erl2005service}, in which the
application is divided into \textit{services} that do not share a database. Each
service has its own, possibly of a different type, so that no coupling exists
between the databases and the different parts of the application. However, all
access to a service must go through the API it exposes, under a centralized
communication middleware: as a consequence, the middleware and the API services
may become bottlenecks that limit the scalability of the system, undermining the
benefits of the isolation introduced by the separation of contexts. 

The \textit{microservices} architecture emerges as a response to the limitations
of both the monolith and SOA, of which it can be regarded as a refinement,
retaining the principle of dividing an application into services with their own
data. Instead of replicating a monolith as a whole, the application is
decomposed into a set of small, independently deployable services, each
responsible for a specific business capability. Rather than routing all
communication through a centralized middleware, services interact through
lightweight messages exchanged directly over the network, so that no single
component constrains the scalability of the whole system. Moreover, the
separation is not only logical but also physical: each service owns its data
store and can run on different machines in a different number of copies. Each
service can therefore be scaled, updated, and recovered independently of the
rest of the system, so that components subject to higher load are replicated
accordingly to their scalability needs, and the failure of a single service does
not necessarily compromise the whole application. \\

The microservices architectural style can therefore be characterized along four
dimensions of isolation that loosely coupled, independently deployable
services aim to achieve. \textit{Isolation of state} is ensured by each
microservice owning and managing its own data, with no shared, mutable state
accessible to other services. \textit{Isolation of space} follows from
services being physically separable and independently deployable, allowing
each one to be scaled on its own, potentially running one or more instances
across different machines. \textit{Isolation of time}, together with
\textit{isolation of failure}, stems from communication between services
typically being asynchronous, decoupling the lifecycle of a request from the
availability of the services involved and from failures being contained
within the boundaries of the service in which they occur, increasing the
overall availability of the system.

Adopting this architecture, however, does not by itself determine how an
application should be divided: a microservice is not a unit of size, but rather
a unit of functionality. For this reason, the microservice
\textit{responsibility} guides the design of the system, follwing the
\textit{single responsibility principle}, originally stated for object-oriented
classes: a class should have only one reason to change. The same principle
applies to microservices, so that a change to the internals of one does not 
necessitate a change to another. A good starting point for identifying such
responsibilities comes from \textit{Domain-Driven Design}
(DDD)~\cite{evans2004domain}, an approach that centers the design of software on
the model of the business domain. Within DDD, a \textit{bounded context} defines
the context in which a specific model applies, with its own terminology and rules. 

Once the boundaries of the services have been defined, the way they behave at
runtime becomes the main concern. The goals pursued by this architecture are
software that scales, consumes only the resources necessary to support the
current load, handles failures with little or no effect on the user, and is
distributed across machines, while maintaining a consistent level of quality and
responsiveness despite all of these aspects. These goals are formalized by the
\textit{Reactive Manifesto}~\cite{reactive-manifesto}, which defines a
\textit{reactive system} through the following four principles
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Responsive}: the system responds in a timely fashion, thereby
    building user confidence. 
    \item \textbf{Resilient}: the system remains responsive even when failures
    occur. By leveraging replication, isolation, containment and delegation,
    failures are confined to a single component without propagating throughout
    the system, while recovery is delegated to an external component. 
    \item \textbf{Elastic}: the system maintains responsiveness despite
    fluctuations in workload. This behavior relies on the absence of contention
    and central bottlenecks, allowing the system to scale up during demand peaks
    to ensure responsiveness and scale down to maximize cost effectiveness,
    often supported by predictive auto-scaling techniques. 
    \item \textbf{Message-driven}: the system relies on asynchronous,
    non-blocking message passing, which establishes loose coupling, isolation
    and location transparency. Consequently, resources are consumed only while
    active, preventing components from remaining blocked since responses are
    also delivered asynchronously. 
\end{itemize}
These four principles are deeply interconnected. The message-driven nature of
the system serves as the foundation upon which the others rest: asynchronous
messages decouple components and grant location transparency, enabling seamless
replication across distributed nodes. Consequently, the system achieves
resilience and handles dynamic operational loads by scaling components up or
down according to current demand, thereby attaining elasticity. Resilience and
elasticity, in turn, sustain system responsiveness. When implemented using
event-driven communication and decentralized data management, microservices
naturally align with this vision, embodying the core properties required by a
reactive system. \\

Microservices are characterized by independent deployment, multiple
independent databases and loose coupling between components, with communication
that can be either synchronous or asynchronous. Deployment is rapid, possibly
continuous and teams release features when they are ready. These
characteristics are usually accompanied by an organizational change: teams,
often organized around a \textit{DevOps} approach, operate more independently,
release cycles become shorter and cross-team coordination is less necessary,
which can increase productivity. Isolation and decoupling also give each team
more flexibility in evolving the internals of its service, and allow multiple
platforms and languages to coexist within the same system.

These benefits come at a cost: deployment and monitoring may require multiple
and complex approaches~\cite{faseeha2025observability}, while refactoring across
services is more challenging than in a monolith, since a change that spans
several services must be coordinated among different teams. The reason behind
this resides in the fact that the complexity that was inside the monolith is now
moved outside the single services, in the coordination among them. As a
consequence, the overall system architecture becomes more complex and a single
request may require information from several services, with the client needing
to know which services exist, who provides what and how to reach them. This
problem is addressed by the introduction of \textit{gateway services}, which
have the role of aggregating requests and responses from multiple services,
hiding the complexity of the architecture from the client. Consequently, service
management becomes a major operational challenge, particularly concerning data
governance: while service independence allows adoption of heterogeneous
technologies tailored to specific domain requirements, it introduces a loss of a
holistic system view, leading to data fragmentation across disparate data
sources. \\

In this context, gateway services typically act as an implementation of the
\textit{facade pattern}~\cite{gamma1994design}, providing a unified and
simplified interface over a set of underlying microservices to obscure system
complexity. However, whenever new data requirements arise, this traditional
approach forces codebase changes on the gateway services and therefore the
following deployment of new versions, introducing operational risks and
potential bugs. To overcome these limitations, \textit{DEX} abstracts the core
tenets of the facade pattern into a fully configuration-driven architecture:
rather than modifying source code, new data aggregation logic and service
integrations are declared through configuration files, thereby adhering to the
principle of code reuse and mitigating software regression risks.

\section{Heterogeneous Data Sources}\label{sec:heterogeneous-data-sources}
An increasing number of modern software systems rely on \textit{distributed
database systems}, which can be defined as an integration of autonomous local
databases that are geographically distributed and interconnected by a network,
effectively controlled by a \textit{distributed database management system
({DDBMS})}~\cite{vikiru2023overview}.

This choice is driven by requirements that a single-instance database can
hardly sustain. First, data must be readily available to users: through
\textit{replication} we are able to improve the \textit{availability} of the
system with copies of the same data stored on multiple nodes, allowing to keep
serving requests even in the presence of hardware or network failures. Moreover,
replication enables to reduces latency, since copies can be placed
geographically close to the clients that access them. Second, the growing volume
of data often exceeds the capacity of a single machine: to address this, the
database is divided into subsets through \textit{fragmentation}. In
\textit{vertical} fragmentation, a dataset is divided based on the attributes of
its records, so that each subset holds only some of the fields of every record.
The choice of which attributes to group together is typically driven by the
properties of the data store and by the goal of optimizing its usage. In
\textit{horizontal} fragmentation instead, the dataset is divided based on its
records, holding complete ones and being mostly adopted when a single machine
cannot handle the amount of data or the load generated by queries. A widespread
form of horizontal fragmentation is \textit{sharding}, in which the subsets,
called \textit{shards}, share the same structure and are distributed across
different nodes, usually by assigning each record to a shard according to the
value of a partition key. \\ 
These strategies are not mutually exclusive: they can be applied together
through \textit{mixed} fragmentation policies and, even if they pursues
different objectives, they all contribute to distributing the load of incoming
requests across the nodes of the system.

Distributed database systems are expected to provide \textit{transparency}, a
property ensured by the \textit{DDBMS} that manages them. Transparency is
defined as the view of a distributed database as a centralised logical view to
the system, which hides the complexities that arises from data being distributed
and the actual implementation of the provided solutions
~\cite{coronel2016database}. In particular, the \textit{DDBMS} conceals how data
is distributed across the different nodes, how it is fragmented and stored at
different locations, and the strategies used to handle the replicated fragments
at various sites. This responsibility of the \textit{DDBMS} is further
articulated along several dimensions~\cite{vikiru2023overview}:
\begin{itemize}
      \item \textit{access transparency}: users cannot differentiate the
      access methods to local or remote databases;
      \item \textit{migration transparency}: users are unaware of the movement
      of system information or processes, as their operations are not
      interfered with;
      \item \textit{scaling transparency}: nodes or resources can be added to
      or removed from the system without affecting normal operations;
      \item \textit{performance transparency}: the system can be reconfigured
      without interfering with user operations;
      \item \textit{database transparency}: users can access data from the
      system databases without knowledge of the underlying commands.
\end{itemize}
To these dimensions we add a further one, which we refer to as
\textit{consistency transparency}: users are unaware of the effort required to
keep data consistent across its replicated copies. 
Indeed, if on one side replication provides many of the benefits discussed
previously, such as improved availability and fault tolerance, on the other it
introduces the main problem regarding data consistency: the copies of the same
data must remain aligned while it is created, modified, and deleted. Ideally,
the \textit{DDBMS} hides this problem entirely, so that users observe the same
data regardless of the copy they access. This ideal is not always achieved:
systems such as \textit{Riak} and \textit{Cassandra} favor availability over
absolute consistency and provide \textit{eventual consistency}, where copies may 
temporarily diverge and converge to the same value only once updates cease to
be propagated. Despite that, the details of how this is achieved go beyond the
concerns of this thesis, since \textit{DEX} is designed as a framework with
read-only access to the data.

The need for transparency that motivates distributed database systems
re-emerges, in a different form, in architectures that adopt heterogeneous
data sources, as it happens in microservices. \\
This design choice stems from the fact that different services, or more
generally different components of a system, may favor different storage
technologies, depending on their functional requirements, on the
characteristics of the data they handle, and on the load they are expected to
sustain, which in turn determines distinct scalability needs. Each family of
data stores, in fact, embodies a different set of trade-offs.
\textit{Relational stores}, with their tabular organization, enforce a strict
data structure and provide reliable transactional guarantees, while SQL allows
the expression of complex queries. However, they suffer limitations in terms of
horizontal scalability, since partitioning leads to hard transactional checks
and distributed joins are costly. Moreover, the rigidity of the schema may
become a drawback when modifications are required, as these often entail
expensive migrations: for this reason, \textit{Document stores}, are conversely
well suited to schema-less or flexible data structures and lend themselves
naturally to horizontal scaling through sharding, at the price of increased data
redundancy and, typically, weaker consistency guarantees, such as \textit{eventual
consistency}. When the workload consists of simple data accessed through a
unique key, \textit{key-value stores} are usually preferred, as they offer
simplicity and excellent scalability, but they provide limited querying
capabilities. Finally, when the system must manage large volumes of data and is
mainly oriented towards read-intensive analytical workloads,
\textit{column-oriented stores} offer highly efficient bulk loading and
analytical queries, requiring on the other hand a data model designed 
around predefined query patterns and with poor performance on single-row
operations. 

With the \textit{database per service} pattern, an application's data is
partitioned not across the nodes of a single, internally coordinated system, but
across multiple, independent data sources. Each of them is owned by a different
service and may adopt a different data model, query language, and storage
technology. In this scenario, no \textit{DDBMS} exists to play the role of a
unifying layer: each data source exposes its own interface and no common
abstraction is available to query across them. This \textit{heterogeneity}
directly undermines the notion of transparency, persued by \textit{DDBMS}s, as a
centralized logical view of distributed databases, which in these systems become
heterogeneous. Indeed, as soon as the boundaries of a single distributed
database are exited and the infrastructure is observed as a whole, this
transparency vanishes. Specifically, from a read-only access data point of view,
\textit{access} and \textit{database} transparency dimensions are no longer
guaranteed. As a consequence, a query spanning multiple services cannot
therefore be expressed as a single operation, but must be decomposed into
subqueries tailored to each data source, whose partial results must then be
reconciled into a coherent answer. 

\textit{DEX} is designed to fill precisely this gap: just as a
\textit{DDBMS} provides a centralized logical view over data distributed
across the nodes of a system, it proposes the same logical view over data
distributed across heterogeneous, independently managed data sources. 
Users can thus access an application's data as a unified whole, regardless of
where it is stored and of the technologies adopted by each individual service.

\section{Data Abstraction and Governance}
Data has become one of the most valuable assets of modern organizations, which
rely on it to support decisions, to operate their products and to create value.
As its importance grows, so does the need to manage it deliberately: this need
falls under \textit{data governance}, defined as the exercise of authority and
control over the management of data, with the purpose of increasing its value
while minimizing data-related cost and risk~\cite{abraham2019data}. From an
organizational perspective, this translates into the allocation of decision
rights and accountability for an organization's data assets, a responsibility
that spans several interrelated domainsz including data quality, metadata,
access, and lifecycle~\cite{khatri2010designing}. 

Governance concerns not only the quality of data, but also who is allowed to
access it, under which conditions and through which means.
As discussed in \autoref{sec:heterogeneous-data-sources}, the transparency
provided by distributed database systems enhance data usability, but this still
require thecnical knowledge to access data with specific technolgies.  This is
not sufficient when access should be granted to everyone who is able to extract
value from the data, regardless of their technical background: from this idea
arises the motivation at the base for \textit{DEX}, not just as tool that can be
used by technical people to query data in a trasparent way regarding where data
resides in relation to the technolgies used, but especially to extend data
exploration accessibility to all those non-tehcnical people. \\
For this reason, abstracting only the heterogeneity of the data sources, while
still exposing to the end user the schema and the technical details of how data
is structured, is just a partial step towards our goal. As a natural evolution
of this idea, \textit{DEX} aims to offer not only an abstraction over the
location and the storage technology of the data, but also over the way in which
data is presented to the end user. 

Not all the data that is stored is meaningful to the end user: as an example,
the database of a service typically contains internal identifiers, audit
fields, synchronization flags, and auxiliary tables that exist only to support
the implementation of the service, while the end user is interested only in
the business information they ultimately represent. \\
Moreover, exposing the data schema may reveal internal solutions
adopted by the organization and make sensitive data reachable. An abstraction
that decouples what is exposed from how data is physically structured protects
such data and prevents unintended leakage. It also enables \textit{schema-agnostic}
queries: users express their information needs in terms of the logical entities
offered by the abstraction, independently of the physical schema. As a
consequence, changes that affect only the physical representation of the data,
without altering its logical meaning, can be absorbed by updating the mapping
between the two levels, leaving the queries expressed by users unaffected.

In addition, since end users are not required to navigate the data sources
directly, \textit{DEX} acts as a \textit{middleware} between users and the
underlying services. This position makes it possible to exercise control over
the data, which would otherwise be bypassed by direct access to the sources.
Every query is mediated by \textit{DEX}, which can evaluate it against the
access policies defined by the organization before any data is retrieved, and
return only what the user is entitled to see. Indeed, as systems grow in scale
and interconnection, for instance in domains such as smart cities and
large-scale \textit{Internet of Things} deployments, the number of parties that
may need to query a subset of an organization's data, internal teams, partner
organizations, or automated systems, grows accordingly, amplifying the risk
associated with uncontrolled data access. With \textit{DEX}, such policies can
be defined once, at the level of the abstraction exposed to users, and apply
uniformly to all the underlying data sources, regardless of how each of them
manages its own permissions. Governance thus becomes a property of the access
path, rather than a responsibility distributed across every individual source,
supporting compliance with both internal policies and external regulations.

\section{Federated Query Processing}
\textit{Federated query processing} is the technique through which a single
query, formulated over a set of autonomous and heterogeneous data sources, is
executed by decomposing it into subqueries, running them independently of one
another and aggregating their partial results into a single response.
The technique makes no assumption on where the subqueries are executed: they may 
run at the data sources themselves or at the processing engine, and, being
independent, they can be carried out in a distributed and parallel fashion.

The fragmentation of data across multiple and independently managed data sources
prevents a query from being expressed as a single operation against a
centralized data store. Two main strategies can be adopted to overcome this
limitation. The first consists in integrating the relevant data into a local,
centralized warehouse and processing queries against this copy: here the data is
moved to the query. The second, known as federated query processing, takes the
opposite direction: rather than moving the data to the query, it brings the
query to the data, leaving information in the sources that own it and decomposing
each query into subqueries executed directly at the source sites.

The warehouse approach can be suboptimal for several reasons. First, it
requires copying data, which introduces storage costs and a replica that must
be kept aligned with the sources. Since the copy is refreshed periodically,
queries may be answered with stale data, which is undesirable when the
information need depends on the current state of the system. Second, it
requires designing and maintaining the pipelines that extract, transform, and
load the data, together with an integrated schema defined in advance. This
effort grows with the number of sources, and must be repeated whenever a
source changes. Third, it removes the data from the control of the service
that owns it, moving it outside the boundaries in which access policies were
defined and creating a further copy to be protected. Finally, it is poorly
suited to exploratory scenarios, where the data of interest is not known in
advance: integrating everything beforehand is costly, while integrating only
what is needed requires knowing it beforehand.

Conversely, federated query processing addresses these limitations by leaving
the data where it is. Since no copy is made, queries are evaluated against the
current state of the sources, so the answers are always fresh and no replica has
to be stored or kept aligned. Moreover, the effort required to integrate a new
source is reduced: instead of building and maintaining extraction,
transformation, and loading pipelines, it is sufficient to describe how the
source maps onto the logical model exposed to the users. This also makes the
approach more robust to change, as a modification of a source affects only the
description of that source rather than the whole integration process. Regarding
access policies, the data remains under the control of the service that owns it,
which continues to enforce its own policies, without additional copies who may
need to be protected. Finally, the approach is well suited to exploratory
scenarios: since nothing has to be integrated beforehand, any source can be
queried as soon as it is made available, and only the data actually requested is
accessed. A further advantage is that independent subqueries can be executed in
parallel, each by the most appropriate engine for its source, exploiting the
capabilities of the underlying systems, something which is valuable in the
presence of heterogeneous data sources. Indeed, each subquery can exploit the
characteristics for which the underlying technology was originally chosen,
preserving the benefits that resides behind those architectural decisions. \\
These advantages come with trade-offs, which the design of a federated system
must take into account. Query performance depends on the responsiveness of the
individual sources and on the cost of transferring partial results, in addition
to the fact that the availability of an answer depends on the availability of
every source involved.

Federated query processing typically proceeds in five stages. First, during
\textit{query parsing}, the user's query is translated into an internal
representation suitable for further processing. Second, during
\textit{query optimization}, the plan that decomposes the original query into
subqueries is produced, together with the strategy with which they
should be executed and combined, typically aiming to minimize execution times
and requests while maximizing the completeness of the final result. Third, during
\textit{data source selection}, the data sources relevant to the query are
identified, based on the schema or capabilities exposed by each source.
Fourth, during \textit{query execution}, the subqueries are dispatched to
their respective data sources and the corresponding partial results are
collected. Finally, during \textit{result aggregation}, these partial results
are combined into a single response, which is finally returned to the user.

However, regarding \textit{query optimization} in the execution of a federated
query, is not merely a matter of dispatching subqueries and combining their
results. A naive decomposition treats each subquery in isolation, so that
filters and constraints that are meaningful for the query as a whole may be
retained in some subqueries and omitted from others. The partial results
returned by the sources are then larger than necessary, and contain a
considerable amount of data that is irrelevant to the final answer, which must
be transferred over the network and subsequently discarded during result
aggregation. As a consequence, a significant share of the computation is
deferred to the aggregation phase, where joins performed over large intermediate
results become particularly costly, both in execution times and memory
consumptions. For this reason, as pointed out in the \textit{FedX} study
~\cite{schwarte2011fedx}, optimization techniques are needed to reduce the number
of intermediate requests and the amount of data shipped from the sources, while
still guaranteeing fast execution of the individual requests. In this direction,
a possible solution consists in enriching the subqueries with additional filters
that reduce the size of partial results at the source, thereby anticipating part
of the work that would otherwise be carried out by the joins during final
aggregation. A similar concern motivates the \textit{exclusive 
groups} proposed in \textit{FedX}, which are introduced precisely because
performing joins at the aggregating server is expensive.

\section{Data Exploration}
\textit{Data Exploration} can be defined as the process through which users
formulate information needs over a dataset even when they do not know exactly
what they were looking for, involving the act of investingating among data,
often realizing that something is interesting only after finding it. Typically,
an exploration session includes several queries, where the result of each one
triggers the formulation of the next one, uncovering unkown or unanticipated
insights in the data, letting information that is needed to emerge and evolve
itself rather than being fully known in advance.  

As stated in~\cite{idreos2015overview}, traditional data management systems
assume that users, when posing a query, have a good knowledge of the data schema
and that the query they provide leads to a result that is actually what they
wanted, presupposing that the end user already knows what they are looking for.
These assumptions are strong: not only they require the user to have a precise
understanding of the schema and semantics of the underlying data, but they
also presuppose that the user has the technical expertise needed to express
even complex queries. Moreover, distributed and heterogeneous data sources
aggravate this situation even further. On one hand, they increase the cognitive
load required of the user, who needs to know not only which data is available,
but also where it resides, a difficulty that becomes even more problematic in
data exploration processes, where we may potentially not know in advance what
information we are looking for. On the other hand, they increase the technical
competencies required to access the data itself, as different sources may expose
different query languages and technologies, such as SQL and NoSQL, each
requiring its own specific expertise simply to access the data at all.

In organizational contexts, however, restricting data exploration just to
personnel used to those query technologies can be a limitation which may exclude
domain experts who, despite lacking a technical background, may hold knowledge
that is highly relevant to bringing value to the work being carried out. For
these users, the requirement to master query technologies becomes an obstacle
not only to data exploration, but more broadly to any attempt to consult
data that is not made available to them or not in the form they would need by
the services provided to them. This situation is commonly addressed in practice
through mediation: whenever a custom, ad-hoc query is required, who needs those
data must rely on the company or department responsible for the software product
to implement it on their behalf. 

This solution, however, requires a certain amount of effort, especially in
organizations where data is distributed across different services owned by
different teams, a common situation in companies that adopt microservice
architecture, adding a coordination effort on top. This process becomes
increasingly costly, not only for the labor required from technical personnel,
but also because it imposes a considerable maintenance burden on development teams,
repeatedly involved in implementing one-off queries that fall outside the
scope of the system's predefined interfaces, diverting development time away
from work that would otherwise be more valuable to the system.
An even more critical consequence, however, concerns timing: this mediation
process introduces significant latency between the formulation of an
information need and its fulfillment, a delay that is particularly
degrading to data exploration. A further factor central to data exploration
is, in fact, interactive time: the delay between the formulation of an
information need and the availability of an answer, or at least a partial
one, sufficient to provide the user with enough context to formulate their
next request. This responsiveness is crucial, as the data exploration process
must remain interactive enough to keep the user engaged and avoid breaking
their train of thought. Distribution, in this regard, introduces additinal
latency, arising not only from the need for different nodes to communicate with
one another, but also from the fact that additional work is required to
coordinate the execution across the distributed environment and to reconcile the
partial results into a single answer. This complexity is unavoidable if we want
to enable a data exploration process that remains as transparent as possible
with respect to how the system is actually structured, a distinction that is
fundamental to support data exploration beyond the problem of data fragmentation
in today's distributed systems, particularly within microservices architectures.

Given the issues discussed in this section, this thesis aims to address
them through the design of \textit{DEX}, guided by a set of concrete objectives.\\
First, \textit{DEX} seeks to abstract users from the schema of the underlying data
sources and from the knowledge of which specific data source a given piece of data
originates from, information which is not relevant to someone engaged in data
exploration and is primarily interested in the data itself rather than in
how or where it is stored. What matters to the user is the data and its meaning,
not whether it is stored in a relational, document-oriented, key-value, graph,
or any other type of data store. \textit{DEX}'s second objective is to prevent
users from the requirement of being proficient in the specific query technology
adopted by each data source, regardless of whether it exposes a structured query
language, such as \textit{SQL} or a \textit{NoSQL} query language, or a
different access paradigm, such as the APIs typically exposed by key-value
stores. Third, \textit{DEX} aims to relieve users from the effort required to
aggregate data retrieved from potentially different sources, providing them
instead with a single, unified response: if this aggregation effort were
left to the user performing the data exploration, it would become a
significant burden, hindering an effective exploration process. \\
Given the centrality of interactive time in data exploration to enable a fluid
user experience, \textit{DEX} further strives to return a valuable response as
quickly as possible, even if the response is temporarily incomplete, providing
users with feedback that can inform and facilitate their exploration process
toward their final objective. \\ 
Taken together, these aims amount to abstracting the user from the complexity
introduced in distributed systems, while also removing when possible the need
for mediation with a technical team, freeing them to focus on work of greater
value to the system and avoiding the intrinsic latency of that mediation
process. When such mediation cannot be entirely avoided, these aims are
nonetheless pursued through a design that keeps the framework easily
maintainable and extensible, able to accommodate new data sources, new
technologies, and changes to the data made available for exploration.
This design is further guided by the goal of keeping the cognitive load imposed
on the user low, allowing data to be discovered, understood, and related to
other data with ease. \\
The overall goal of \textit{DEX} is therefore to facilitate data exploration,
all that while trying to remain efficient in terms of both system load and query
performance, making organization's data more broadly accessible to those who can 
leverage it to bring value.

\section{Existing Solutions}
To the best of our knowledge, no existing solution directly addresses the
problem of providing both schema-agnostic query abstraction and centralized
data governance over heterogeneous data sources belonging to independent
microservices, making our approach novel in this specific combination. Several
existing tools, however, address parts of this problem space, and are worth
discussing to better position our contribution.

\textit{jQuery QueryBuilder} is a client-side plugin that allows users to
build queries visually and export them in several formats. Its query model is
based on a logic of filters combined through \textit{AND}/\textit{OR}
conditions, an idea that was adopted in our framework and extended: instead of
stopping at filters, \textit{DEX} introduces the concept of \textit{Object}
and of relations between objects, making it possible to express queries that
span multiple entities. Moreover, \textit{jQuery QueryBuilder} reproposes the
schema of the underlying database to the user, rather than providing a further
layer of abstraction over it, as done in \textit{DEX}. Finally, it is only a
query construction tool and offers no engine for executing the queries it
produces.

\textit{Visual Query Builder} and \textit{WeaveBird} offer an interesting visual
representation of queries, based on flows of connected diagrams in which the
filters are linked to one another. This representation was partially adopted in
\textit{DEX} to convey the logical flow in which filters are chained.
However, in these tools the filters are evaluated one after the other simply as
\textit{AND} conditions, without the possibility of expressing \textit{OR}
conditions, which is instead supported in \textit{DEX}.

\textit{Item Query} is a framework that allows users to run successive queries
on previously obtained results, applying further filters to progressively
refine them. This idea was adopted in \textit{DEX}, since it is well suited to
\textit{data exploration}: users rarely know in advance exactly which data they
need, and tend to approach it iteratively, starting from a broad question and
narrowing it down as they inspect intermediate results. Reusing a previous
result as the starting point of a new query allows this process to proceed
incrementally, without having to rewrite the whole query from scratch at every
step, and lets users build their understanding of the data step by step.

Among the tools considered, \textit{Metabase} and \textit{FedX}
~\cite{schwarte2011fedx} are the closest point of comparison. 
\textit{Metabase} exposes the schemas of connected databases to end users, allowing
them to interact with their data through a \textit{no-code} interface, without
explicitly writing query code. However, \textit{Metabase} operates directly on
top of the exposed database schemas, rather than introducing an additional
abstraction layer over them. This limits its applicability in two ways relevant
to this thesis: it does not decouple the user-facing query model from the
physical schema of each underlying source, and it does not natively support the
kind of cross-source query decomposition and reconciliation required by federated
query processing, as it is primarily designed to query one connected data
source at a time.

Regarding \textit{FedX}, it comes as a federated query processing tool: a query
is decomposed into subqueries, which are dispatched to the relevant data sources
and whose partial results are then combined into a single answer. As with
\textit{DEX}, it recognize that the cost of this process is largely determined
by the amount of intermediate data shipped to the aggregating component, and
that joins performed there are expensive, leading to the introduction of
optimizations that are reproposed in \textit{DEX}, such as \textit{exclusive
groups}. However, important differences arises concearning the context of the
our framework and \textit{FedX}: \textit{FedX} targets \textit{Linked Data},
where every source exposes an RDF dataset through a \textit{SPARQL} endpoint:
the sources share the same data model and query language, so heterogeneity is
largely absent at this level, and users are expected to write \textit{SPARQL}
queries directly against the vocabularies of the sources. \textit{DEX}, instead,
operates on sources that differ in data model, query language, and storage
technology, belonging to independent microservices. Furthermore, \textit{DEX}
introduces an abstraction layer, based on \textit{Objects} and on the relations
between them, as previously mentioned for \textit{Metabase}.

Across all these solutions, the combination of goals pursued by this thesis,
a schema-agnostic abstraction over heterogeneous data sources, usable by
non-technical end users and serving as a natural enforcement point for data
governance policies, is not addressed as a whole by any single existing
tool, positioning our framework as an original contribution to this problem
space.
