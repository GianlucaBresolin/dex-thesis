\section{Data Exploration}
\textit{Data Exploration} can be defined as the process through which users
formulate information needs over a dataset even when they do not know exactly
what they were looking for, involving the act of investingating among data,
often realizing that something is interesting only after finding it. Typically,
an exploration session includes several queries, where the result of each one
triggers the formulation of the next one, uncovering unkown or unanticipated
insights in the data, letting information that is needed to emerge and evolve
itself rather than being fully known in advance.  

As stated in \cite{idreos2015overview}, traditional data management systems
assume that users, when posing a query, have a good knowledge of the data schema
and that the query they provide leads to a result that is actually what they
wanted, presupposing that the end user already knows what they are looking for.
These assumptions are strong: not only they require the user to have a precise
understanding of the schema and semantics of the underlying data, but they
also presuppose that the user has the technical expertise needed to express
even complex queries. Moreover, distributed and heterogeneous data sources
aggravate this situation even further. On one hand, they increase the cognitive
load required of the user, who needs to know not only which data is available,
but also where it resides, a difficulty that becomes even more problematic in
data exploration processes, where we may potentially not know in advance what
information we are looking for. On the other hand, they increase the technical
competencies required to access the data itself, as different sources may expose
different query languages and technologies, such as SQL and NoSQL, each
requiring its own specific expertise simply to access the data at all.

In organizational contexts, however, restricting data exploration just to
personnel used to those query technologies can be a limitation which may exclude
domain experts who, despite lacking a technical background, may hold knowledge
that is highly relevant to bringing value to the work being carried out. For
these users, the requirement to master query technologies becomes an obstacle
not only to data exploration, but more broadly to any attempt to consult
data that is not made available to them or not in the form they would need by
the services provided to them. This situation is commonly addressed in practice
through mediation: whenever a custom, ad-hoc query is required, who needs those
data must rely on the company or department responsible for the software product
to implement it on their behalf. 

This solution, however, requires a certain amount of effort, especially in
organizations where data is distributed across different services owned by
different teams, a common situation in companies that adopt microservice
architecture, adding a coordination effort on top. This process becomes
increasingly costly, not only for the labor required from technical personnel,
but also because it imposes a considerable maintenance burden on development teams,
repeatedly involved in implementing one-off queries that fall outside the
scope of the system's predefined interfaces, diverting development time away
from work that would otherwise be more valuable to the system.
An even more critical consequence, however, concerns timing: this mediation
process introduces significant latency between the formulation of an
information need and its fulfillment, a delay that is particularly
degrading to data exploration. A further factor central to data exploration
is, in fact, interactive time: the delay between the formulation of an
information need and the availability of an answer, or at least a partial
one, sufficient to provide the user with enough context to formulate their
next request. This responsiveness is crucial, as the data exploration process
must remain interactive enough to keep the user engaged and avoid breaking
their train of thought. Distribution, in this regard, introduces additinal
latency, arising not only from the need for different nodes to communicate with
one another, but also from the fact that additional work is required to
coordinate the execution across the distributed environment and to reconcile the
partial results into a single answer. This complexity is unavoidable if we want
to enable a data exploration process that remains as transparent as possible
with respect to how the system is actually structured, a distinction that is
fundamental to support data exploration beyond the problem of data fragmentation
in today's distributed systems, particularly within microservices architectures.

Given the issues discussed in this section, this thesis aims to address
them through the design of \textit{DEX}, guided by a set of concrete objectives.\\
First, \textit{DEX} seeks to abstract users from the schema of the underlying data
sources and from the knowledge of which specific data source a given piece of data
originates from, information which is not relevant to someone engaged in data
exploration and is primarily interested in the data itself rather than in
how or where it is stored. What matters to the user is the data and its meaning,
not whether it is stored in a relational, document-oriented, key-value, graph,
or any other type of data store. \textit{DEX}'s second objective is to prevent
users from the requirement of being proficient in the specific query technology
adopted by each data source, regardless of whether it exposes a structured query
language, such as \textit{SQL} or a \textit{NoSQL} query language, or a
different access paradigm, such as the APIs typically exposed by key-value
stores. Third, \textit{DEX} aims to relieve users from the effort required to
aggregate data retrieved from potentially different sources, providing them
instead with a single, unified response: if this aggregation effort were
left to the user performing the data exploration, it would become a
significant burden, hindering an effective exploration process. \\
Given the centrality of interactive time in data exploration to enable a fluid
user experience, \textit{DEX} further strives to return a valuable response as
quickly as possible, even if the response is temporarily incomplete, providing
users with feedback that can inform and facilitate their exploration process
toward their final objective. \\ 
Taken together, these aims amount to abstracting the user from the complexity
introduced in distributed systems, while also removing when possible the need
for mediation with a technical team, freeing them to focus on work of greater
value to the system and avoiding the intrinsic latency of that mediation
process. When such mediation cannot be entirely avoided, these aims are
nonetheless pursued through a design that keeps the framework easily
maintainable and extensible, able to accommodate new data sources, new
technologies, and changes to the data made available for exploration.
This design is further guided by the goal of keeping the cognitive load imposed
on the user low, allowing data to be discovered, understood, and related to
other data with ease. \\
The overall goal of \textit{DEX} is therefore to facilitate data exploration,
all that while trying to remain efficient in terms of both system load and query
performance, making organization's data more broadly accessible to those who can 
leverage it to bring value.


\section{Heterogeneous Data Sources}
An increasing number of modern software systems rely on \textit{distributed
database systems}, which can be defined as an integration of autonomous local
databases that are geographically distributed and interconnected by a network,
effectively controlled by a \textit{distributed database management system
({DDBMS})} ~\cite{vikiru2023overview}.

This choice is driven by requirements that a single-instance database can
hardly sustain. First, data must be readily available to users: through
\textit{replication} we are able to improve the \textit{availability} of the
system with copies of the same data stored on multiple nodes, allowing to keep
serving requests even in the presence of hardware or network failures. Moreover,
replication enables to reduces latency, since copies can be placed
geographically close to the clients that access them. Second, the growing volume
of data often exceeds the capacity of a single machine: to address this, the
database is divided into subsets through \textit{fragmentation}. In
\textit{vertical} fragmentation, a dataset is divided based on the attributes of
its records, so that each subset holds only some of the fields of every record.
The choice of which attributes to group together is typically driven by the
properties of the data store and by the goal of optimizing its usage. In
\textit{horizontal} fragmentation instead, the dataset is divided based on its
records, holding complete ones and being mostly adopted when a single machine
cannot handle the amount of data or the load generated by queries. A widespread
form of horizontal fragmentation is \textit{sharding}, in which the subsets,
called \textit{shards}, share the same structure and are distributed across
different nodes, usually by assigning each record to a shard according to the
value of a partition key. \\ 
These strategies are not mutually exclusive: they can be applied together
through \textit{mixed} fragmentation policies and, even if they pursues
different objectives, they all contribute to distributing the load of incoming
requests across the nodes of the system.

Distributed database systems are expected to provide \textit{transparency}, a
property ensured by the \textit{DDBMS} that manages them. Transparency is
defined by \cite{coronel2016database} as the view of a distributed database as a
centralised logical view to the system, which hides the complexities that arises
from data being distributed and the actual implementation of the provided
solutions. In particular, the \textit{DDBMS} conceals how data is distributed 
across the different nodes, how it is fragmented and stored at different
locations, and the strategies used to handle the replicated fragments at
various sites. This responsibility of the \textit{DDBMS} is further articulated
along several dimensions~\cite{vikiru2023overview}:
\begin{itemize}
      \item \textit{access transparency}: users cannot differentiate the
      access methods to local or remote databases;
      \item \textit{migration transparency}: users are unaware of the movement
      of system information or processes, as their operations are not
      interfered with;
      \item \textit{scaling transparency}: nodes or resources can be added to
      or removed from the system without affecting normal operations;
      \item \textit{performance transparency}: the system can be reconfigured
      without interfering with user operations;
      \item \textit{database transparency}: users can access data from the
      system databases without knowledge of the underlying commands.
\end{itemize}
To these dimensions we add a further one, not mentioned in the cited paper,
which we refer to as \textit{consistency transparency}: users are unaware of
the effort required to keep data consistent across its replicated copies.
Indeed, if on one side replication provides many of the benefits discussed
previously, such as improved availability and fault tolerance, on the other it
introduces the main problem regarding data consistency: the copies of the same
data must remain aligned while it is created, modified, and deleted. Ideally,
the \textit{DDBMS} hides this problem entirely, so that users observe the same
data regardless of the copy they access. This ideal is not always achieved:
systems such as \textit{Riak} and \textit{Cassandra} favor availability over
absolute consistency and provide \textit{eventual consistency}, where copies may 
temporarily diverge and converge to the same value only once updates cease to
be propagated. Despite that, the details of how this is achieved go beyond the
concerns of this thesis, since \textit{DEX} is designed as a framework with
read-only access to the data.

The need for transparency that motivates distributed database systems
re-emerges, in a different form, in architectures that adopt heterogeneous
data sources, as it happens in microservices. \\
This design choice stems from the fact that different services, or more
generally different components of a system, may favor different storage
technologies, depending on their functional requirements, on the
characteristics of the data they handle, and on the load they are expected to
sustain, which in turn determines distinct scalability needs. Each family of
data stores, in fact, embodies a different set of trade-offs.
\textit{Relational stores}, with their tabular organization, enforce a strict
data structure and provide reliable transactional guarantees, while SQL allows
the expression of complex queries. However, they suffer limitations in terms of
horizontal scalability, since partitioning leads to hard transactional checks
and distributed joins are costly. Moreover, the rigidity of the schema may
become a drawback when modifications are required, as these often entail
expensive migrations: for this reason, \textit{Document stores}, are conversely
well suited to schema-less or flexible data structures and lend themselves
naturally to horizontal scaling through sharding, at the price of increased data
redundancy and, typically, weaker consistency guarantees, such as \textit{eventual
consistency}. When the workload consists of simple data accessed through a
unique key, \textit{key-value stores} are usually preferred, as they offer
simplicity and excellent scalability, but they provide limited querying
capabilities. Finally, when the system must manage large volumes of data and is
mainly oriented towards read-intensive analytical workloads,
\textit{column-oriented stores} offer highly efficient bulk loading and
analytical queries, requiring on the other hand a data model designed 
around predefined query patterns and with poor performance on single-row
operations. 

With the \textit{database per
service} pattern, an application's data is partitioned not across the nodes of
a single, internally coordinated system, but across multiple, independent data
sources. Each of them is owned by a different service and may adopt a different
data model, query language, and storage technology. In this scenario, no
\textit{DDBMS} exists to play the role of a unifying layer: each data source
exposes its own interface and no common abstraction is available to query
across them. This \textit{heterogeneity} directly undermines the notion of
transparency, persued by \textit{DDBMS}s, as a centralized logical view of
distributed databases, which in these systems become heterogeneous. Indeed, as
soon as the boundaries of a single distributed database are exited and the
infrastructure is observed as a whole, this transparency vanishes. Specifically,
from a read-only access data point of view, \textit{access} and
\textit{database} transparency dimensions are no longer guaranteed. As a 
consequence, a query spanning multiple services cannot therefore be expressed as
a single operation, but must be decomposed into subqueries tailored to each data
source, whose partial results must then be reconciled into a coherent answer.

\textit{DEX} is designed to fill precisely this gap: just as a
\textit{DDBMS} provides a centralized logical view over data distributed
across the nodes of a system, it proposes the same logical view over data
distributed across heterogeneous, independently managed data sources. 
Users can thus access an application's data as a unified whole, regardless of
where it is stored and of the technologies adopted by each individual service.
